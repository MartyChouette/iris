01 — Concept

A contemplative life sim about
preparation and consequence.

Spent Blooms is a contemplative life sim about preparing for dates and living with the consequences. You play as Nema, alone in a small apartment. Each morning a newspaper arrives with personal ads. You choose someone to invite over, then spend the day making the apartment worthy of them — wiping stains, arranging objects, choosing music, watering plants, pouring drinks. Every choice is physical, unhurried, and tactile.

Their affection — rendered as a growing flower — is the sum of your preparation. Afterward, you trim that flower. The trimmed bloom joins a growing bouquet in your refrigerator.

When your date arrives, they notice everything. They judge your outfit at the door, react to the drink you pour, wander the living room reading your shelves and reacting to your plants.

Over seven days, the calendar fills. Dates accumulate. Some of them go very well. Those are the ones who stop calling back.

03 — Core Loop

One day. Four phases.
One flower.

  1. Morning
    Newspaper Arrives
    • Read personal ads — scan for highlighted keywords
    • Choose tonight's date
  2. Afternoon
    Preparation Phase — Timed
    • Clean stains, arrange objects, water plants
    • Select vinyl, spray perfume, prepare drinks
    • Set the apartment exactly how you want it seen
  3. Evening
    The Date Arrives
    • Phase 1 — Entrance: outfit, perfume, greeting, cleanliness judged
    • Phase 2 — Kitchen: make their drink from memory or guesswork
    • Phase 3 — Living Room: they investigate everything you placed
  4. Night
    The Aftermath
    • Date ends — a flower is left behind
    • Flower trimming scene: cut the stems, score the arrangement
    • Trimmed flower joins the growing bouquet in the fridge
    • Sleep — next morning begins

    Each person visits three times. After the third date — the one with the strange flower — they don't call back. A souvenir appears the next morning.

04 — Key Mechanics

Six interlocking systems
that share one emotional register.

01
Physical Apartment Preparation

Every object is a physics prop. You pick things up, carry them, place them. The watering can needs to be filled and tilted. The vinyl record must be extracted from its sleeve and walked to the turntable. Drink bottles pour in real time with layered liquid physics. The preparation phase is not a menu — it is the game.

02
The Date's Gaze

Your date notices specific things in a specific order. Phase 3 is an item-by-item reveal: the date walks to each object, pauses, reacts. A thought bubble appears — heart, neutral face, or frown — while a multiplier popup shows how much the item mattered.

03
The Flower as Score

Every date leaves a flower. You trim it in a standalone scene using virtual scissors — cutting angle, stem length, and condition all feed into a score. The trimmed bloom joins a growing bouquet in the refrigerator — a physical record of every date you've had. The first two are beautiful. The third is... different.

04
The Keyword System

Every character has preferences that surface as highlighted keywords across the newspaper, dialogue, and item descriptions.

jazzterracottabookbindingfermentationsyntheticclutter

The system rewards close reading. Attention paid to a personal ad on day one pays off in affection on day three.

05
The Mood Machine

Weather, time of day, music, plant health, and air quality all feed into a single 0–1 mood value. This drives light color, ambient tone, fog density, rain particles, and audio mix in real time. A stormy evening with a dead plant and no music feels different from a clear sunset with jazz and a healthy fern.

06
The Horror Layer — Woven In, Never Explained

Each person visits three times. You learn their preferences, refine your preparation, deepen the romance. Their third flower arrives wrong — wilted, dark, thorned. After that final date, they vanish. A personal item appears in the apartment. Every player will notice people stop showing up. The horror isn't hidden — it's unavoidable. What's optional is how deeply you read into it. The game never explains Nema. The player decides what it means.

05 — Aesthetic & Tone

Cozy first. Mystery always.
Dread only when it counts.

Visual Style

PS2-era low-poly with warm URP rendering. Soft vertex snapping, affine texture warping, and 2D dithering give the game a distinctly retro texture while the lighting and color grading remain modern and intentional.

Tone Balance
60% COZY25% MYSTERY & FUN15% CREEPY & SEXY

The apartment sim is genuine. The observation layer is playful detective work. The romance lingers. The rare moments of dread land hard because the rest of the game earned your trust.

Audio Identity

The record player is a core game object — vinyl selection directly affects the apartment's mood. Original lo-fi and jazz tracks scored for each in-game record. Sound design is tactile: the click of a bottle cap, water filling a can, scissors on a stem.

Palette

Muted, warm. Cream walls, terracotta pots, soft greens, amber lamplight. The PSX aesthetic keeps the game feeling hand-made rather than procedural.

07 — Why This Game

Five things Spent Blooms does
that no other game does.

  • Cozy, romantic, and unsettling in the same scene.The horror is never delivered by a cutscene or jump scare. It emerges from the same systems the cozy gameplay and romance run on. The mechanics are the dread.
  • The bouquet as a living record.Every trimmed flower joins a growing bouquet in the refrigerator. Open the fridge and your dating history stares back at you.
  • Two marketing cycles from one game.Launch sells a cozy apartment dating sim. Post-launch discovery of the horror layer drives a second wave of content creator coverage.

A1 No dialogue trees — environmental storytelling as a design & financial advantage
›
Current Framing

Not currently in the pitch. The Keyword System is described as a mechanic but the "no dialogue trees" stance isn't called out as a deliberate design choice.

Proposed Framing

Every other dating sim runs on branching dialogue. Spent Blooms learns everything about a character through their personal ad, their reactions to your apartment, and the souvenir they leave behind. Zero dialogue trees. This is a narrative differentiator AND a production efficiency win.

Examples & Evidence
  • Narrative: What you learn about someone is inferred from signals, not stated. Closer to Golden Idol than Coffee Talk.
  • Production: No 30,000-line VO-ready dialogue scripts. Lower content cost, faster iteration, cheaper localization.
  • Replay: Signals are ambient — re-reading an ad after meeting the person means different things.
  • Marketing: "The dating sim with no dialogue" is a headline-worthy hook.
Interactive Demo — Hover a keyword
Seeking someone patient who loves jazz records and fermentation. Allergic to synthetic scents. My apartment runs warm; I prefer terracotta over clean lines.

This is the entire "dialogue system." Four highlighted keywords convey every preference the player needs — no branching trees, no voice lines, no conversation UI. Hover a keyword to see the gameplay implication.

A2 Nema is a protagonist, not a self-insert
›
Current Framing

Nema is mentioned as "alone in a small apartment." The game "never explains Nema." But Nema isn't positioned as a narrative differentiator — she reads as a neutral viewpoint character.

Proposed Framing

99% of dating sims use blank-slate protagonists for player projection. Nema has her own pathology, her own apartment, her own agency in what's happening. You're not roleplaying yourself on dates — you're inhabiting a specific, strange person.

Examples & Evidence
  • Emotional stakes: The question "am I doing this?" has weight because Nema is a person, not a camera.
  • Art direction: You see Nema in the apartment doing idle things (kitchen, couch, mirror) — she's present, not invisible.
  • Precedent: Like Omori, Life is Strange, Spiritfarer — games where the protagonist's specific identity is the whole hook, not a customization layer.
  • Writing: Nema has idle animations, a bathroom reflection, a disco ball moment. She is the game's main visual presence.
A3 Three-act structure per character — every date is a short story
›
Current Framing

Mentioned in passing: "Each person visits three times." The three-visit structure is described mechanically but not as a narrative or commercial differentiator.

Proposed Framing

Not one-and-done (Coffee Talk). Not infinite (Stardew). Every date character is a three-act short story. Date 1: first impression. Date 2: deepening. Date 3: the dark ending. A deliberate structure, not a treadmill.

Examples & Evidence
  • Reading experience: Like a short story collection — each person is a complete arc.
  • Pacing: 3 visits guarantees a crescendo — the 3rd flower is always wrong.
  • Scope: 7 characters × 3 visits = 21 encounters. Finite, shippable, reviewable.
  • Replay: Different character orderings create different emotional shapes of the 7-day calendar.

A4 Physical verbs, not buttons — make this the stated design pillar
›
Current Framing

"Fully physical UI" and "preparation IS the game" are in USPs. But physicality is treated as a feature, not the design thesis that binds every system.

Proposed Framing

Every interaction has a visual body. This is the philosophy that makes cozy preparation feel real AND the horror feel implicating. It's the thread that runs through every mechanic. Frame it as the design pillar, not just a feature.

Examples & Evidence
  • Feel: Pouring a drink isn't clicking "serve" — it's tilting a bottle, watching liquid layer.
  • Implication: Because you physically acted, you're implicated when the date disappears.
  • Differentiator: Coffee Talk, Dream Daddy, Monster Prom — all button-based. Spent Blooms is verb-based.
  • Marketing: Every physical verb is a 2-second GIF for social media.
Interactive Demo — Click a verb
Pour
Tilt a bottle over a glass. Layered liquid physics mean each drink has visible cross-section cocktails that accumulate in real time. Mess up the order, and the date can see it through the glass.
A5 You are implicated, not witnessing
›
Current Framing

The horror layer says "the player decides what it means." True, but passive. Positions the player as an observer.

Proposed Framing

You chose the ad. You set the apartment. You poured the drink. You trimmed the flower. The mechanical structure asks "did I do this?" rather than "what happened?" No comparable cozy game takes this stance — even Spiritfarer makes loss happen TO you.

Examples & Evidence
  • Agency = culpability: Every verb you perform before a disappearance rereads as evidence.
  • Contrast: Spiritfarer = you receive souls. Spent Blooms = you select them.
  • Emotional payload: Content creators visibly wrestle with this framing — exactly the reaction the game wants.
  • Marketing: "The cozy game that asks if you're the villain" is a line that prints money.
A6 Timed preparation — tension is a cozy feature, not a flaw
›
Current Framing

The afternoon is labeled "Timed" in the Core Loop but the timing isn't sold as a feature — it reads as a constraint.

Proposed Framing

Most cozy games are stress-free. Spent Blooms has real time pressure during prep. That tension creates stakes that make preparation matter and makes the cozy moments feel earned. Cozy-with-bite, not cozy-as-meditation.

Examples & Evidence
  • Rhythm: Morning deliberation → afternoon sprint → evening performance. Three distinct emotional speeds per day.
  • Tension: Watching the clock while deciding between watering a plant OR extracting a vinyl record.
  • Precedent: Overcooked / Diner Dash showed timed prep is addictive; Spent Blooms slows it down but preserves the stakes.
  • Accessibility: Can expose a "casual" timer mode for players who want zero pressure.
